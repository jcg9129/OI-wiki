Many concepts of three-dimensional geometry are common with [two-dimensional geometry](./2d.md); we can use the same method as solving two-dimensional geometry problems to solve three-dimensional geometry problems.

## Basic concepts

The concepts of points, vectors, and lines are similar to two-dimensional geometry, and are not elaborated here.

### Plane

We can use a point $P_0(x_0,y_0,z_0)$ on the plane and the normal vector of that plane (i.e. the vector perpendicular to that plane) $\boldsymbol{n}$ to represent a plane.

Because $\boldsymbol{n}$ is perpendicular to the plane, $\boldsymbol{n}$ is perpendicular to all lines in that plane. In other words, let $\boldsymbol{n}=(A,B,C)$; then all points $P(x,y,z)$ on that plane satisfy $\boldsymbol{n} \cdot \overrightarrow{PP_0} = 0$.

By the definition of the vector dot product, the above expression is equivalent to:

$$
A(x-x_0)+B(y-y_0)+C(z-z_0)=0
$$

After simplification:

$$
Ax+By+Cz-(Ax_0+By_0+Cz_0)=0
$$

Let $D=-(Ax_0+By_0+Cz_0)$; then the above expression becomes $Ax+By+Cz+D=0$. We call this expression the **general form** of the plane.

## Basic operations

### Angles between lines and planes

Using the knowledge of space vectors, the angles between lines and planes in space can be quickly found.

For two skew lines $a$, $b$, through a point $P$ in space, draw $a' \parallel a$, $b' \parallel b'$; then the acute angle or right angle formed by $a'$ and $b'$ is called the **angle formed by the two skew lines** $a$ and $b$.

For a line $a$ and a plane $\alpha$, if $a$ intersects $\alpha$ at $A$, and through a point $P$ on $a$ we draw a perpendicular to plane $\alpha$ intersecting $\alpha$ at $O$, then the complementary angle of the angle formed by $a$ and $PO$ is called the **angle formed by the line and the plane**. In particular, if $a \parallel \alpha$ or $a \subset \alpha$, then the angle between them is $0^\circ$.

For two planes $\alpha$, $\beta$, their angle is defined as the angle formed by two lines $a,b$ (where $a \subset \alpha$, $b \subset \beta$) perpendicular to the intersection line $l$ of the two planes.

#### Definition of the angle between two lines and the necessary-and-sufficient conditions of relations

-   The angle between the direction vectors of two lines is called the angle between the two lines.

With this proposition, we can obtain the following conclusion: given two lines $l_1, l_2$ whose direction vectors are $s_1 (m_1, n_1, p_1)$, $s_2 (m_2, n_2, p_2)$ respectively, let $\varphi$ be the angle between the two lines; we can obtain $\cos \varphi = \dfrac{\left | m_1m_2+n_1n_2+p_1p_2 \right |}{\sqrt{m_1^2+n_1^2+p_1^2}\sqrt{m_2^2+n_2^2+p_2^2}}$.

-   $l_1 \perp l_2 \iff m_1m_2 + n_1n_2 + p_1p_2 = 0$

-   $l_1 \parallel l_2 \iff \dfrac{m_1}{m_2} = \dfrac{n_1}{n_2} = \dfrac{p_1}{p_2}$.

### The angle between a three-dimensional vector and a plane

When a line is not perpendicular to a plane, the angle $\varphi$ ($\varphi \in [0, \frac{\pi}{2}]$) between the line and its projected line on the plane is called the angle between the line and the plane.

Let the line vector be $s(m, n, p)$ and the plane normal vector be $f(a, b, c)$; then the following propositions hold:

-   The sine of the angle: $\sin\varphi = \dfrac{\left | am + bn + cp \right |}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}\sqrt{m^2+n^2+p^2}}$

-   The line is parallel to the plane $\iff am+bn+cp = 0$

-   The line is perpendicular to the plane $\iff \dfrac{a}{m} = \dfrac{b}{n} = \dfrac{c}{p}$

## Theorems of solid geometry

### Triple sine theorem

Let the degree of the dihedral angle $M－AB－N$ be $\alpha$; on plane $M$ there is a ray $AC$ forming an angle $\beta$ with the edge $AB$ and forming an angle $\gamma$ with plane $N$; then $\sin\gamma = \sin\alpha\cdot\sin\beta$.

### Triple cosine theorem

Let $O$ be a point on a plane; the projection of the line $BO$ through a point $B$ outside the plane onto the plane is $AO$, and $OC$ is a line on the plane; then the cosine relationship of the three angles $\angle COB, \angle AOC, \angle AOB$ is: $\cos\angle BOC=\cos\angle AOB\cdot\cos\angle AOC$ ($\angle AOC$, $\angle AOB$ can only be acute angles).
