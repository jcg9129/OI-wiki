When studying, we usually place figures in the Cartesian coordinate system or the polar coordinate system, which makes solving problems much more convenient.

## Recording figures

### Points

In the Cartesian coordinate system, a point is represented by its x- and y-coordinates, such as the point $(5,2)$, the point $(-1,0)$, etc. Since the coordinates are an ordered pair, they can be stored using [`std::pair`](https://en.cppreference.com/w/cpp/utility/pair.html), or a struct can be defined for storage.

In the polar coordinate system, a point is represented by its polar radius and polar angle, and can be stored using a storage method similar to the Cartesian coordinate system.

### Vectors

Since the coordinate representation of a vector is the same as a point, we only need to store a vector like a point.

In the polar coordinate system, it is the same as for a point.

### Lines

Representing lines in the polar coordinate system is relatively difficult, so we only discuss the case in the Cartesian coordinate system.

#### Lines and rays

Generally, when solving mathematical problems, we use an analytic expression to represent a line. The analytic expression of a line has many forms such as the general form $Ax+By+C=0$, the slope-intercept form $y=kx+b$, the intercept form $\dfrac{x}{a}+\dfrac{y}{b}=1$, etc., but its form ultimately serves solving equations for analytic solutions. In solving some computational geometry problems, using an analytic expression to represent a line increases the difficulty of solving the problem.

In computational geometry, we usually use a point on the line and the direction vector of the line to represent a line, and use the endpoint of a ray and the direction vector of the ray to represent a ray. The direction vector is a nonzero vector parallel to the line, usually taken as a unit vector. There are two unit vectors parallel to a line, and one can choose either according to the problem; while for a ray, the direction vector usually takes the one whose direction is the same as the extension direction of the ray.

#### Line segments

Recording a line segment only requires recording its left and right endpoints.

### Polygons

Just use an array to record each vertex of the polygon in a certain order. For a simple polygon, the vertices are generally recorded in clockwise or counterclockwise order.

Specially, when recording a rectangle, if all sides of the rectangle are parallel to the coordinate axes, we only need to record the two vertices on its diagonal.

### Curved figures

Some special curves, such as function graphs, are generally recorded by their analytic expression. For a circle, we directly record its center and radius.

## Basic formulas

### Law of sines

In $\triangle \text{ABC}$, if the sides opposite angles $A,B,C$ are $a,b,c$ respectively, then:

$$
\frac{a}{\sin A}=\frac{b}{\sin B}=\frac{c}{\sin C}=2R
$$

where $R$ is the circumradius of $\triangle \text{ABC}$.

### Law of cosines

In $\triangle \text{ABC}$, if the sides opposite angles $A,B,C$ are $a,b,c$ respectively, then:

$$
\begin{aligned}
a^2&=b^2+c^2-2bc\cos A\\
b^2&=a^2+c^2-2ac\cos B\\
c^2&=a^2+b^2-2ab\cos C
\end{aligned}
$$

The proofs of the above formulas are omitted. They are all content from the People's Education Press high-school mathematics A version compulsory book two (compulsory book five in the old textbook).

## Basic operations

### Determining which side of a line a point is on

We have a point $P$ on the line and the direction vector $\mathbf v$ of the line, and want to know which side of the line a certain point $Q$ is on. The "which side" here is relative to the direction vector, that is, when judging we face the direction the direction vector points and judge.

We use the property of the cross product to compute $\mathbf v\times \overrightarrow {PQ}$. By the right-hand rule, if the cross product is negative, then $Q$ is on the right side of the line; if the cross product is $0$, then $Q$ is on the line; if the cross product is positive, then $Q$ is on the left side of the line.

### Bounding-box test and straddle test

We now want to determine whether two line segments intersect.

First, special-case some special situations. If two line segments are parallel, they naturally cannot intersect. This case can be judged by checking whether the slopes of the lines where the segments lie are equal.

Of course, if the two segments overlap or partially overlap, or the intersection point of the two segments is an endpoint of one of the segments, we only need to judge whether there is a case of three collinear points.

There are also some obviously non-intersecting situations, which we colloquially call "the two segments are too far apart". Define "the region of a line segment" as the region occupied by the rectangle that has this segment as its diagonal and all of whose sides are parallel to some coordinate axis; then it can be found that if two segments have no common region, then these two segments must not intersect.

For example, there are the following two segments:

The regions they occupy are like this:

So we can quickly determine that these two segments do not intersect.

This is the **bounding-box test**. The above situation is called **failing the bounding-box test**.

Failing the bounding-box test is a **sufficient but not necessary condition** for two segments to have no intersection point; we still need further judgment.

Because for two intersecting segments $a,b$, the two endpoints of segment $b$ must be distributed on both sides of the line where segment $a$ lies; similarly, the two endpoints of segment $a$ must be distributed on both sides of the line where segment $b$ lies. We can directly judge the positional relationship of the two endpoints of one segment relative to the line where the other segment lies; if different, then the two segments intersect, otherwise they do not intersect. As said in the previous section, the positional relationship between a line and a point can be judged using the cross product.

This is the **straddle test**; if for two segments $a,b$, the two endpoints of segment $b$ are distributed on both sides of the line where segment $a$ lies, **and** the two endpoints of segment $a$ are distributed on both sides of the line where segment $b$ lies, then we say the two segments $a,b$ **passed the straddle test**, i.e. the two segments intersect.

Note that when two segments are collinear but do not intersect, they can also pass the straddle test, so to judge accurately it still needs to be combined with the bounding-box test.

### Determining whether a point is inside a polygon

In computational geometry, this problem is called the [PIP problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_in_polygon), and there are already some mature solution methods, introduced in turn below.

#### Ray casting algorithm

You can see the most original idea [here](https://wrfranklin.org/Research/Short_Notes/pnpoly.html).

We first special-case some special situations, such as "this point is too far from the polygon". Similar to judging whether two segments intersect, consider the smallest rectangle that can completely cover this polygon; if this point is not within this rectangle's range, then this point must not be inside the polygon. Such a rectangle is easy to find; we only need to know the minimum and maximum of the polygon's x-coordinates and y-coordinates, and combine the coordinates pairwise into four points, which are the four vertices of this rectangle.

There is also the case where the point is on some side or some vertex of the polygon, which is likewise very easy to judge. For each edge, just check whether the point is on the edge or on a vertex.

We consider drawing a ray with this point as the endpoint; if this ray has an odd number of intersection points with the polygon, then this point is inside the polygon, otherwise this point is outside the polygon; we abbreviate it as **odd inside, even outside**. This algorithm is also called the Even-odd rule.

By the [Jordan curve theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan_curve_theorem), we know that each time this ray intersects an edge of the polygon, it switches the inside-outside relationship with the polygon once, so we just count the parity of the number of intersection points.

For the chosen ray, one can randomly choose the slope of the line where this ray lies; an irrational number is recommended to avoid the case where the ray coincides with some edge of the polygon.

In the original code, the last point in the array recording the polygon is used as a point on the ray; in this way, when counting, if the ray passes through some edge or some vertex of the polygon, one can stipulate that the points the ray passes through are on the same side of the ray, and then do the straddle test.

???+ warning "Note"
    The ray casting algorithm only applies to simple polygons. For polygons with self-intersecting edges, the Jordan curve theorem does not apply, so it cannot judge.

#### Winding number algorithm

The winding number is a mathematical concept, the total number of times a closed curve in the plane winds counterclockwise around this point. It is easy to find that when the winding number equals $0$, the point is outside the curve. This algorithm is also called the Nonzero-rule.

How to compute it? We connect this point with all vertices of the polygon, and compute the sum of the angles between adjacent edges. Note that the angle here is **directed**. If the sum of angles is $0$, then this point is outside the polygon, otherwise it is inside the polygon.

### Finding the intersection point of two lines

First, we need to determine that the two lines intersect; just judge whether the direction vectors of the two lines are parallel. If the direction vectors are parallel, then the two lines are parallel, and the number of intersection points is $0$. Further, if the two lines are parallel and pass through the same point, then the two lines coincide.

Then, the problem is simplified to: we have lines $AB,CD$ intersecting at one point, and want to find the intersection point $E$.

If two lines intersect, then there is only one intersection point; we recorded a point on the line and the direction vector of the line, so we only need to know the distance $l$ between this point and the intersection point, and then translate this point along the direction vector by $l$ unit lengths.

Consider constructing a triangle and using the law of sines to solve for $l$; we can construct the law of sines using the cross product.

![Intersection](./images/2d-intersection.svg)

From the figure above, $|\mathbf a\times \mathbf b|=|\mathbf a||\mathbf b|\sin \beta$, $|\mathbf u\times \mathbf b|=|\mathbf u||\mathbf b|\sin \theta$.

Taking the quotient:

$$
T=\frac{|\mathbf u\times \mathbf b|}{|\mathbf a\times \mathbf b|}=\frac{|\mathbf u|\sin \theta}{|\mathbf a|\sin \beta}
$$

We can see that $\left|\dfrac{|\mathbf u|\sin \theta}{\sin \beta}\right|=l$. If the expression inside the absolute value is positive, it represents translating along the $\mathbf a$ direction, otherwise the opposite direction.

At the same time, we directly multiply $T$ by $\mathbf a$, and the unit vector of the line automatically appears, without needing other elimination operations.

So, we only need to subtract $T\mathbf a$ from point $B$ to obtain the intersection point.

### Finding the perimeter and area of an arbitrary polygon

#### Finding the perimeter of an arbitrary polygon

Just compute directly; conciseness is a virtue.

#### Finding the area of an arbitrary polygon

Considering the geometric meaning of the magnitude of the cross product, we can complete it using the cross product.

Label the points on the polygon counterclockwise as $p_1,p_2,\cdots ,p_n$, then choose any auxiliary point $O$, and denote the vector $\mathbf v_i=p_i-O$; then the area $S$ of this polygon can be expressed as:

$$
S=\frac{1}{2}\left|\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf v_i\times \mathbf v_{(i\bmod n)+1}\right|
$$

### Circle-and-line related

#### Finding the intersection points of a line and a circle

First judge the positional relationship between the line and the circle. If the line and the circle are separate, there is no intersection point; if tangent, we can use the tangent line to find the tangent point and the line where the radius lies, and then convert it to finding the intersection point of two lines.

If there are two intersection points, then we can use the Pythagorean theorem to find the midpoint of the two intersection points, then add the half chord length along the line direction.

#### Finding the intersection points of two circles

First we judge the positional relationship between the two circles; if externally separate or internally contained, there is no intersection point; if tangent, we can compute the direction vector of the line connecting the two centers, then use the two circle radii to compute the translation distance, and finally translate the center along this direction vector.

If the two circles intersect, then there must be two intersection points, symmetric about the line connecting the two centers. Therefore below we only explain the method of finding one intersection point; the other intersection point can be found by a similar method.

We first connect the center of one circle with the intersection point, and find the angle formed between the line connecting the two centers and this connecting line. In this way, rotating the direction vector of the line connecting the two centers by this angle gives the direction vector of the radius formed by connecting the center with the intersection point. Translating the center by the radius length along the direction vector gives an intersection point.

## Polar angle order

Generally speaking, this kind of problem needs to first enumerate a pole, then compute the polar coordinates of the other points, and solve the problem in polar-angle order in the polar coordinate system.

???+ example "[「JOISC 2014 Day4」The Constellation of Two People](https://loj.ac/p/2882)"
    There are $n$ points in the plane, with three colors, and the color of each point is one of the three. Find the number of pairs of non-intersecting three-color triangles. $6\le n\le 3000$.

??? note "Solution"
    If two triangles do not intersect, then two internal common tangents can always be drawn; if they intersect or are internally contained, internal common tangents cannot be drawn. The common tangents of triangles can be analogized to the common tangents of circles.
    
    First enumerate an origin, denoted $O$; take this point as the pole, and the line passing through this point and parallel to the $x$-axis as the polar axis, to establish a polar coordinate system, and sort the remaining points by polar angle from small to large. Then count the number of each kind of point above and below the polar axis.
    
    Then enumerate the common tangent according to points; denote the enumerated point as $P$, and initially the common tangent is the polar axis. Start counting. Then there must be a common tangent passing through point $O$ and point $P$. Because the common tangent does not intersect the triangle, one side chooses points above the common tangent, and the other side must choose points below. Then use the multiplication principle to count the number of schemes.
    
    After counting, rotate the common tangent; then point $P$ must have changed its up-down position relative to the common tangent, while the other points do not move, so we should only change its position information. In this way, we can find that the same pair of triangles is finally counted $4$ times, i.e. the same common tangent is enumerated twice, so the final answer should be divided by $4$.
    
    Analyze the algorithm complexity. We enumerated an origin, then for each origin, sorted the remaining points and counted linearly, so the time complexity is $O(n^2\log n)$.

## Notes on writing code

Since computational geometry often performs floating-point computations of the `double` type, it brings precision problems and time problems.

Some problems, such as finding the area of a triangle whose point coordinates are all integers, can use their special nature to perform pure integer computation, avoiding using floating point which affects precision.

Since the reading and computation of floating-point numbers are both slower than integers, we need to note the impact of the program's constant factor on time.
