## Introduction

**Group theory** mainly studies the [algebraic structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_structure) of groups.

To study the structure of groups, we need to master some basic tools, including subgroups, group homomorphisms, and group actions. In algorithm competitions, the groups mainly involved are groups related to number theory (such as the multiplicative group of integers modulo $n$, $(\mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z)^\times$) and permutation groups; this article will focus on the related concepts. For the parts of group theory not covered in this article, such as the structure theory of finite groups and the linear representation theory of groups, interested readers should refer to professional books.

???+ info "Notation"
    When no ambiguity arises, this article may write $g\cdot h$ as $gh$, and may write the group $(G,\cdot)$ as the group $G$.

Understanding abstract algebra cannot be separated from examples. As an example for understanding the concepts below, here we discuss the space symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle.

???+ example "Example: the space symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle"
    As shown in the figure, for a given equilateral triangle, there are a total of six different operations that can make it coincide with itself.
    
    ![The space symmetry group D\_6 of an equilateral triangle](../images/triangle_d6.svg)
    
    Here, we use $r$ to denote clockwise rotation, and $s$ to denote flipping along the line from vertex $1$ to the center of the triangle; operations are composed from right to left, i.e. $sr$ denotes first rotating ($r$) and then flipping ($s$). Two operations are different if and only if some vertex of the triangle is in a different position after the two operations.
    
    | Notation | Operation | Order | Permutation representation |
    | :----: | :-----------------: | :-: | :-----: |
    | $e$ | identity transformation, i.e. doing nothing | 1 | $(1)$ |
    | $r$ | clockwise rotation by $120^\circ$ | 3 | $(123)$ |
    | $r^2$ | clockwise rotation by $240^\circ$ | 3 | $(132)$ |
    | $s$ | flip along the line from $1$ to the center | 2 | $(23)$ |
    | $sr$ | first rotate $120^\circ$, then flip | 2 | $(13)$ |
    | $sr^2$ | first rotate $240^\circ$, then flip | 2 | $(12)$ |
    
    It is easy to verify that these operations do constitute a group. For example, the identity element of this group is $e$, and the inverse element of $sr$ is itself. Moreover, the group $D_6$ is not a commutative group; for example we can directly verify $rs=sr^{-1}$.
    
    The operations recorded in the table are not the only symmetry operation corresponding to that notation. For example, "flip along the line from $2$ to the center" is also a symmetry operation of the triangle; it is not in the table, but its result is the same as the operation "first rotate $120^\circ$, then flip". The concepts of "order" and "permutation representation" in the table will be explained below.

## Subgroups

To understand the structure of a given group, we can first analyze its substructures. The substructures of a group are those subsets of the group that still constitute a group under the same operation. From this, we have the following definition.

???+ abstract "Subgroup"
    For a group $(G,\cdot)$ and one of its subsets $H\subseteq G$, if $(H,\cdot)$ is also a group, then the subset $H$ is called a **subgroup** of $G$, denoted $H\le G$.

???+ example "Example: the space symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    In $D_6$, it is easy to verify that its subgroups are $\{e\}$, $\{e,s\}$, $\{e,sr\}$, $\{e,sr^2\}$, $\{e,r,r^2\}$, and $D_6$ itself, six in total. Except for the group $D_6$, these subgroups all have a simpler structure and contain part of the information about the original group.

To determine whether a given subset $H\subseteq G$ is a subgroup, we do not need to verify the definition of a group one by one: the associative property naturally holds; for a subset to become a subgroup, we only need to guarantee that it is closed under the binary operation, has an identity element, and is closed under taking inverses. In fact, these conditions can be summarized together.

???+ note "Theorem (subgroup criterion)"
    A subset $H$ of a group $G$ is a subgroup if and only if, for all elements $g,h \in H$, $g^{-1}h\in H$.

### Subgroup generated by a subset

In general, given a subset $S$ in a group $G$, starting from the elements in $S$ and repeatedly performing multiplication and inversion operations finitely many times, the set of all results that can be obtained becomes a subgroup of $G$. This is called the subgroup generated by the subset $S$.

???+ abstract "Subgroup generated by a subset"
    For a group $G$ and its non-empty subset $S\subseteq G$, if $H$ is the smallest (by inclusion) among the subgroups of $G$ containing $S$, then the subgroup $H$ is called the **subgroup generated by a subset $S$**, and is denoted $\langle S\rangle$. In particular, if $S=\{x\}$ is a singleton set, then $\langle S\rangle$ is also denoted $\langle x\rangle$, called the cyclic subgroup of the powers of the element $x$.

We can prove that for any such subset $S$, we can always find such a subgroup: $\langle S\rangle$ can be constructed as the intersection of all subgroups of $G$ containing $S$.

???+ example "Example: the space symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    Selecting the rotation operation $r$ in the group $D_6$, and repeatedly applying it and its inverse operation, we obtain the subgroup $\{e,r,r^2\}$. It can be denoted $\langle r\rangle$. All non-trivial subgroups in the group $D_6$ can be generated by selecting a certain operation.

The subgroups generated by some subsets are the group itself. Such subsets are especially special, called generating sets of the group.

???+ abstract "Generating set of a group"
    If a subset $S\subseteq G$ of a group $(G,\cdot)$ satisfies $\langle S\rangle=G$, then $S$ is called a **generating set** of $G$. The elements in a generating set $S$ are called **generators**.

A group is a trivial generating set of itself. A more interesting situation is when the generating set is far smaller than the scale of the group itself.

???+ example "Example: the space symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    We can verify that $D_6=\langle s,r\rangle$; that is, any symmetry operation of an equilateral triangle can be obtained through the composition of rotation and flipping.

### Cyclic group

The structure of a group generated by only one element is very simple. Such a group is called a cyclic group.

???+ abstract "Cyclic group"
    For a group $G$, if there exists $x\in G$ such that $G=\langle x\rangle$, then $G$ is called a **cyclic group**.

???+ example "Example: the space symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    All non-trivial subgroups of $D_6$ as well as $\{e\}$ are cyclic groups.

We can prove that the structure of a cyclic group is uniquely determined by its size. If a cyclic group is infinite, then it must have the same group structure as the additive group of integers $(\mathbf Z,+)$, denoted $C_\infty$ or $\mathbf Z$; otherwise, denote the number of elements of the group as $n\in\mathbf N_+$, then it must have the same group structure as the additive group of congruence classes of integers modulo $n$, $(\mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z,+)$, denoted $C_n$ or $\mathbf Z_n$. The rigorous statement of this conclusion requires the concept of [group isomorphism](#group-isomorphism) below, which rigorously describes the fact that two group structures are the same.

???+ note "Classification theorem of cyclic groups"
    A finite cyclic group $G$ of size $n$ is isomorphic to $C_n$; an infinite cyclic group $G$ is isomorphic to $C_\infty$.

??? note "Proof"
    Given a cyclic group $G=\langle x\rangle$, it can always be written as $G=\{x^n:n\in\mathbf Z\}$. If the group $G$ is finite, then there must exist natural numbers $n<m$ satisfying $x^n=x^m$; by the cancellation law, we can obtain $x^{m-n}=e$. At this time, it is useful to take the smallest positive integer $n\in\mathbf N_+$ such that $x^n=e$; then the sequence $\{x^k\}$ will be a cycle of length $n$, and the elements within the period are all distinct (otherwise it violates the minimality of $n$). At this time, the mapping $x^k\mapsto\bar k$ provides an isomorphism $G\rightarrow\mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z$, i.e. $G\cong C_n$. Conversely, if the group $G$ is infinite, then the elements in the group $G$ are all distinct, and the mapping $x^k\mapsto k$ provides an isomorphism $G\rightarrow\mathbf Z$, i.e. $G\cong C_\infty$.

All cyclic groups are Abelian groups. The example $D_6$ in this article shows that even if all non-trivial subgroups of a group are cyclic groups, the group itself may not be an Abelian group.

### Order

The order of a group is the number of elements of the group. The order of a given element in a group is the order of the cyclic subgroup generated by that element. From this, we have the following definitions:

???+ abstract "Order of a group"
    The **order** of a group $G$ is its number of elements, denoted $|G|$. The order of an infinite group is also infinite.

???+ abstract "Order of an element"
    The **order** of an element $x\in G$ in a group $G$ is the smallest positive integer $n$ such that $x^n=e$ holds, denoted $|x|$; if such an $n$ does not exist, then the order of the element $x$ is said to be infinite, denoted $|x|=\infty$.

The order of an element is always no greater than the order of the group; in fact, as we prove below, the order of an element always divides the order of the group. But, the order of a group is not always the maximum of the orders of the elements; for example, $D_6$ is a group of order six, but the maximum order of an element is $3$. The order of a group is also not the least common multiple of the orders of all elements; for example, the order of the Klein four-group[^klein] $V_4$ is $4$, but it only has elements of order $1$ and order $2$.

???+ note "Theorem"
    In a finite cyclic group $C_n=\langle x\rangle$, the order of the element $x^k$ is
    
    $$
    \frac{n}{\gcd(k,n)}.
    $$
    
    In particular, the number of generators of $C_n$ is $\varphi(n)$, where $\varphi(\cdot)$ is the [Euler function](../number-theory/euler-totient.md).

As an application of the above discussion, note that the order of the multiplicative group of integers modulo $n$ is $\varphi(n)$, and the order of any element $a$ in the group divides it, so we must have $a^{\varphi(n)}=1$. This is [Euler's theorem](../number-theory/fermat.md#eulers-theorem), because the elements in this group are exactly all elements coprime to $n$.

### Coset

The structure of elements outside a subgroup in the group is also not disordered.

???+ example "Example: the space symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    Observing the subgroup $\langle r\rangle$ in $D_6$, the structure of the remaining elements $\{s,sr,sr^2\}$ is very similar to $\langle r\rangle$: each operation in it can be obtained by composing $s$ and an operation in $\langle r\rangle$. Similarly, considering the subgroup $\langle s\rangle$, the remaining elements in the group can be divided into two classes, $\{r,sr\}$ and $\{r^2,sr^2\}$, which can be obtained by composing elements in $\langle s\rangle$ with $r$ and $r^2$ respectively. Such a phenomenon is universal.

Given a subgroup, we can define its cosets.

???+ abstract "Coset"
    Let $G$ be a group and $H\le G$ be its subgroup; then the **left coset** and **right coset** of the subgroup $H$ containing $g$ are defined as the sets
    
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    gH &= \{gh:h\in H\},\\
    Hg &= \{hg:h\in H\}.
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    
    The elements in a coset are called representative elements of the coset.

A subgroup itself is also its coset. Given a subgroup, all cosets constitute a partition of the group, i.e. the group is the disjoint union of all cosets. A partition can always be regarded as the equivalence classes of some equivalence relation. For the partition into left cosets, this equivalence relation is $g_1\sim g_2$ if and only if $g_1^{-1}g_2\in H$; for the partition into right cosets, this equivalence relation is $g_1\sim g_2$ if and only if $g_1g_2^{-1}\in H$.

???+ example "Example: the space symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    In the language of cosets, in the above example, $D_6$ can be partitioned into $\langle r\rangle\cup s\langle r\rangle$ and $\langle s\rangle\cup \langle s\rangle r \cup \langle s\rangle r^2$ respectively. Here, the former partitions the group into several left cosets, and the latter partitions the group into several right cosets. Note that the choice of representative element is not special; for example we can verify $s\langle r\rangle=sr\langle r\rangle$. Any element in a coset is a representative element of that coset.

Different cosets of the same subgroup all have the same size, all equal to the size of the corresponding subgroup. Since all cosets of a given subgroup constitute a partition of the group, the order of a finite group is necessarily an integer multiple of the order of the subgroup. This is called Lagrange's theorem.

???+ note "Lagrange's theorem"
    For a finite group $G$ and its subgroup $H\le G$, $|G|=[G:H]|H|$ holds, where $[G:H]$ denotes the number of left (right) cosets of the subgroup $H$ in $G$, called the **index** of the subgroup $H$ in the group $G$.

??? note "Proof"
    Examine the mapping $h\mapsto gh$ of left-multiplication by $g$; then it and the mapping $h\mapsto g^{-1}h$ are inverse mappings of each other, so they are both bijections. This shows that $|H|=|gH|$ always holds.

Note that the order of an element is the order of the cyclic subgroup generated by the element, so the order of an element also necessarily divides the order of the group.

### Normal subgroup

In general, the left and right cosets of a given subgroup are not the same.

???+ example "Example: the space symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    In the group $D_6$, $\langle s\rangle r=\{r,sr\}$, but $r\langle s\rangle=\{r,sr^2\}$. But if we consider the subgroup $\langle r\rangle$, then the left and right cosets are always the same, because at this time the group has only two cosets, and the subgroup as a coset must coincide.

Whether the left and right cosets are the same reflects the properties of the corresponding subgroup.

???+ abstract "Normal subgroup"
    Let $N\le G$ be a subgroup of the group $G$; if for all $h\in N$ and $g\in G$, $ghg^{-1}\in N$, in other words, for all $g\in G$, $gNg^{-1}\subseteq N$, then $N$ is called a **normal subgroup** of $G$, denoted $N\trianglelefteq G$.

The condition in this definition is exactly equivalent to $gN=Ng$ always holding. A group $G$ always has trivial normal subgroups, i.e. $\langle e\rangle$ and $G$ itself.

???+ example "Example: the space symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    In the group $D_6$, $\langle s\rangle$ is not a normal subgroup, while $\langle r\rangle$ is a normal subgroup.

### Quotient group

Normal subgroups are a very important class of subgroups; one of the reasons is that based on normal subgroups we can define quotient groups.

For a group $G$ and its normal subgroup $N\trianglelefteq G$, consider the set of all cosets

$$
G/N = \{gN:g\in G\}.
$$

At this time, the left and right cosets are the same and do not need to be distinguished. Starting from the operation of the group $G$, we can define a binary operation $\circ$ on $G/N$ satisfying

$$
g_1N\circ g_2N=(g_1g_2)N.
$$

We can prove that the result of the operation is independent of the choice of representative element[^quotient]. At this time, $(G/N,\circ)$ does have the structure of a group, which is called the **quotient group** of $G$ modulo $N$. The quotient group $G/N$ is not a subgroup of the group $G$; each of its elements is a subset of the group $G$.

???+ example "Example: the space symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    In the group $D_6$, the meaning of the quotient group $G/\langle r\rangle$ is very obvious. It is equivalent to, among all these symmetry operations, ignoring the angle by which the operation rotates the triangle, and only paying attention to whether it flips the triangle. The composition of two operations that flip the triangle is equivalent to not flipping the original triangle; but, if of two operations one flips the triangle and the other does not, then the composition necessarily flips the triangle. Whether it flips also has the structure of a group. Ignoring the details of rotation in the group $D_6$ and only considering the presence or absence of flipping is, algebraically, modding the complex group $D_6$ by $\langle r\rangle$ to obtain the quotient group $D_6/\langle r\rangle$. These arguments are invalid for the subgroup $\langle s\rangle$, because if we ignore the presence or absence of flipping, then the degree of rotation cannot be clearly determined, which is also why $G/\langle s\rangle$ does not have the structure of a quotient group.

The quotient group of a group can simplify a complex group, allowing us to observe part of the structure of the group to understand the structure of the original group. This is also the reason why a quotient group is also called a **factor group**. A group that contains no normal subgroups other than the trivial normal subgroups is called a **simple group**; these groups cannot be simplified into smaller groups. Like prime numbers, they are the building blocks that compose more complex group structures.

## Group homomorphism

The second method for understanding the structure of a given group is to compare the structures of two groups with each other.

For two groups, to compare their structures is to construct a mapping between the two groups. But, such a mapping cannot be arbitrary; it must preserve the structure of the group, i.e. it must keep the operations of the group consistent before and after the mapping. Such a mapping is called a group homomorphism.

???+ abstract "Group homomorphism"
    Let the mapping $\varphi:G\rightarrow H$ be a mapping from the group $(G,\cdot)$ to the group $(H,\odot)$; if $\varphi$ preserves the group operation, i.e. for all $g_1,g_2\in G$, $\varphi(g_1\cdot g_2)=\varphi(g_1)\odot\varphi(g_2)$, then the mapping $\varphi$ is called a **homomorphism** from the group $G$ to the group $H$.

A group homomorphism necessarily maps the identity element to the identity element, and necessarily maps an inverse element to an inverse element.

???+ info "Notation"
    In the following text, if no ambiguity arises, we will not distinguish the notation for the operations in groups $G$ and $H$, and for brevity of expression, we will omit these notations.

### Group isomorphism

For a homomorphism $\varphi:G\rightarrow H$ from the group $G$ to the group $H$, a natural question is to what extent this homomorphism reflects that the structures of group $G$ and group $H$ are consistent. For this, examine the image $\varphi(G)$ of the group $G$ under the homomorphism $\varphi$, which maps the structure of the group $G$ into the group $H$. On one hand, $\varphi(G)$ is a subgroup of the group $H$; but if the homomorphism $\varphi$ is not surjective, $\varphi(G)$ is not the same as the group $H$. On the other hand, the homomorphism $\varphi$ is not necessarily injective; if $\varphi$ is not injective, then $\varphi(G)$ only reflects part of the structure of the group $G$. Only when the homomorphism $\varphi$ is bijective are the structures of group $G$ and group $H$ completely consistent. This special group homomorphism is called a group isomorphism.

???+ abstract "Group isomorphism"
    Let $\varphi:G\rightarrow H$ be a homomorphism from the group $G$ to the group $H$; if $\varphi$ is bijective, then $\varphi$ is called an **isomorphism** between the group $G$ and the group $H$, denoted $G\cong H$.

Two isomorphic groups have completely consistent structures. If we only care about the structure of a group, there is no need to distinguish two isomorphic groups. The classification theorem of cyclic groups mentioned earlier is given in the sense of isomorphism.

???+ example "Example: the space symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    Returning to the previous example, each symmetry operation of an equilateral triangle uniquely corresponds to a permutation operation on the vertex set. All permutations on the vertex set also constitute a group, i.e. $S_3$. It is easy to verify that the mapping $\varphi:D_6\rightarrow S_3$ obtained this way is a group homomorphism; furthermore, it is also a group isomorphism. So, $D_6\cong S_3$. In fact, a group of order six is either isomorphic to $C_6$ or isomorphic to $S_3$ (see the proof below).

Classifying the structure of finite groups of a given order is an important research content of group theory, but is beyond the scope of this article.

### Kernel of a homomorphism

For a general homomorphism, we can further discuss how much information about the structure of the group is lost in the homomorphism. Continuing the notation above. We know $\varphi(G)$ is a subgroup of the group $H$; now the key of the problem lies in the relationship between $\varphi(G)$ and the group $G$.

???+ example "Example: the space symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    Consider the mapping $\varphi: D_6 \rightarrow C_2 = \langle x\rangle$ defined as follows:
    
    $$
    \varphi(e)=\varphi(r)=\varphi(r^2)=e,\ \varphi(s)=\varphi(sr)=\varphi(sr^2)=x.
    $$
    
    It is easy to verify that $\varphi$ is a group homomorphism; it is surjective but not injective. Its meaning is obvious, namely mapping each symmetry operation in the group to the presence or absence of its flipping. The information in the group $D_6$ that such a homomorphism compresses is the information about its rotation angle. For example, if there is no flipping, rotation of any angle is mapped to the identity element in the group $C_2$.

This example inspires using the preimage of $\{e\}$ to measure the structural information lost in the homomorphism. For this, we have the following definition.

???+ abstract "Kernel of a homomorphism"
    Let $\varphi:G\rightarrow H$ be a homomorphism from the group $G$ to the group $H$; then the **kernel** of the homomorphism $\varphi$ is $\ker\varphi=\{g\in G:\varphi(g)=e\}$, where $e$ is the identity element of $H$.

### Fundamental theorem of group homomorphism

The kernel $\ker\varphi$ of a homomorphism does characterize the structural information lost in a group homomorphism. The precise statement of this conclusion is the **fundamental theorem of group homomorphism** (also called the **first isomorphism theorem**).

???+ note "Fundamental theorem of group homomorphism (first isomorphism theorem)"
    Let $\varphi:G\rightarrow H$ be a homomorphism from the group $G$ to the group $H$; then $\ker\varphi\trianglelefteq G$, and $G/\ker\varphi\cong\varphi(G)\le H$.

??? note "Proof"
    First, $N=\ker\varphi$ is a normal subgroup, because for any $h\in N$, $\varphi(ghg^{-1})=\varphi(g)\varphi(h)\varphi(g)^{-1}=\varphi(g)\varphi(g)^{-1}=e$, i.e. $ghg^{-1}\in\ker\varphi$. Then, examine the mapping $\Phi:G/N\rightarrow\varphi(G)$ satisfying $\Phi(gN)=\varphi(g)$. The mapping is well-defined, because if $g_1N=g_2N$, then $g_1^{-1}g_2\in N$, so $\varphi(g_1^{-1}g_2)=e$, i.e. $\varphi(g_1)=\varphi(g_2)$. The mapping $\Phi$ is obviously surjective; it is also injective, because $\ker\Phi=\{gN:\varphi(g)=e\}=\{N\}$. Therefore, $\Phi$ is a group isomorphism. Finally, $\varphi(g_1)\varphi(g_2)^{-1}=\varphi(g_1g_2^{-1})\in\varphi(G)$, and by the subgroup criterion, $\varphi(G)$ must be a subgroup.

???+ note "Corollary"
    A homomorphism $\varphi:G\rightarrow H$ is injective if and only if $\ker\varphi=\{e\}$. At this time, $G$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of $H$, i.e. $G\cong\varphi(G)\le H$.

That is to say, the kernel of the homomorphism $\varphi$ is a normal subgroup of the group $G$, and the quotient group $G/\ker\varphi$ obtained by modding $\ker\varphi$ is isomorphic to the image $\varphi(G)$ of the homomorphism, and this image of the homomorphism is exactly a subgroup of the group $H$.

???+ example "Example: the space symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    The kernel of the group homomorphism $\varphi: D_6 \rightarrow C_2$ given above is $\langle r\rangle$; when discussing normal subgroups earlier we have also shown that $D_6/\langle r\rangle$ is indeed isomorphic to $C_2$.

### Natural homomorphism

Obtaining such a conclusion is not surprising. This is because when constructing the homomorphism $\varphi: D_6 \rightarrow C_2$, what is used is exactly the geometric meaning of the quotient group $D_6/\langle r\rangle$. Such a phenomenon is not rare. In fact, for each quotient group, we can construct a group homomorphism such that the image of the homomorphism is isomorphic to the given quotient group.

???+ abstract "Natural homomorphism"
    For a group $G$ and its normal subgroup $N\trianglelefteq G$, the mapping $\pi: G\rightarrow G/N$ given by $\pi(g)=gN$ is a surjective homomorphism from $G$ to $G/N$, called the **natural homomorphism** or **natural mapping** from the group $G$ to the quotient group $G/N$.

This conclusion also shows that for any normal subgroup of a given group, we can find a corresponding group homomorphism such that the kernel of this homomorphism is exactly the given normal subgroup. The fundamental theorem of group homomorphism above shows that the kernel of any homomorphism is a normal subgroup. Therefore, normal subgroups and kernels of homomorphisms are two sides of the same coin.

Using the concept of the natural mapping, the fundamental theorem of group homomorphism is actually saying that the following **commutative diagram** holds.

![Commutative diagram of the fundamental theorem of group homomorphism](../images/homomorphism.svg)

Here, all arrows are group homomorphisms, and $N=\ker\varphi$ is the kernel of the homomorphism $\varphi$, and $\varphi(G)$ is the image of the homomorphism $\varphi$. These mappings are, in order, $\pi:g\mapsto gN$ being the natural mapping (surjective homomorphism) from the group $G$ to the quotient group $G/N$, $\Phi:gN\mapsto\varphi(g)$ being an isomorphism, and $\iota$ being the embedding mapping (injective homomorphism). The commutative diagram means that the results of composing the mappings on the two different paths from $G$ to $H$ in the diagram are consistent, i.e. $\varphi=\iota\circ\Phi\circ\pi$. The commutative diagram clearly shows that the information lost by the homomorphism $\varphi$ is reflected in $\pi$ and $\iota$.

### Isomorphism theorems of groups

A powerful tool for understanding the structure of groups is the isomorphism theorems of groups. The first isomorphism theorem has been given above. For completeness of content, here we give the other common isomorphism theorems.

The second isomorphism theorem involves the concept of the product of subgroups.

???+ abstract "Product of subsets"
    For a group $G$ and its subsets $A,B\subseteq G$, the **product** of the subsets $A$ and $B$ is the subset $AB=\{ab:a\in A,b\in B\}$.

The product of subgroups is not always a subgroup. For example, the product of the subgroup $A=\langle s\rangle$ and the subgroup $B=\langle sr\rangle$ of the group $D_6$ equals $AB=\{e,s,r,sr\}$, which is not a subgroup of $G$, because $(sr)s=r^2\notin AB$. In fact, such a counterexample of the form $a\in A$, $b\in B$, but $ba\notin AB$ is exactly the fundamental reason why the product is not a subgroup. For this, we have the following theorem.

???+ note "Theorem"
    For a group $G$ and its subgroups $A,B\le G$, the product $AB$ is a subgroup if and only if $AB=BA$.

??? note "Proof"
    If the product $AB$ is a subgroup, then $ba=(a^{-1}b^{-1})^{-1}\in AB$ for any $a\in A$ and $b\in B$, so $BA\subseteq AB$. Conversely, if $AB=BA$, then for any $a_1,a_2\in A$ and $b_1,b_2\in B$, $(a_1b_1)(a_2b_2)^{-1}=a_1b_1b_2^{-1}a_2^{-1}\in a_1BA=a_1AB=AB$, so by the subgroup criterion, $AB$ must be a subgroup.

The **second isomorphism theorem** (a.k.a. diamond isomorphism theorem) gives a simpler sufficient condition for the product of subgroups to still be a subgroup, and further determines its structure.

???+ note "Second isomorphism theorem"
    Let a group $G$ and subgroups $A,B\le G$ satisfy $A\le N_G(B)$; then, $AB\le G$, and $B\trianglelefteq AB$, $A\cap B\trianglelefteq A$, $AB/B\cong A/(A\cap B)$. Here, $N_G(B)$ is the [normalizer](#normalizer-and-centralizer) of $B$. In particular, a sufficient condition for $A\le N_G(B)$ is $B\trianglelefteq G$.

??? note "Proof"
    Because $A\le N_G(B)$, we must have $aBa^{-1}=B$ for all $a\in A$, i.e. $aB=Ba$. Therefore, we must have $AB=BA$, so by the above theorem $AB$ is a subgroup. The subgroup $B$, as a subgroup of $AB$, has the same left and right cosets, so $B\trianglelefteq AB$.
    
    Examine the mapping $\varphi:A\rightarrow AB/B$ satisfying $\varphi(a)=aB$; then it is surjective, and its kernel $\ker\varphi=\{a\in A:aB=B\}=A\cap B$. Applying the fundamental theorem of group homomorphism completes the proof.

The **third isomorphism theorem** gives the correspondence between the normal subgroups and quotient groups of a quotient group and the normal subgroups and quotient groups of the original group. It explains the rationality of the idea of further decomposing a quotient group.

???+ note "Third isomorphism theorem"
    Let a group $G$ have normal subgroups $H,K\trianglelefteq G$ with $H\le K$; then $K/H\trianglelefteq G/H$, and $(G/H)/(K/H)\cong G/K$.

??? note "Proof"
    Examine the mapping $\varphi:G/H\rightarrow G/K$ satisfying $\varphi(gH)=gK$; then it is a surjective group homomorphism, and $\ker\varphi=\{gH:g\in K\}=K/H$. Applying the fundamental theorem of group homomorphism completes the proof.

This conclusion can be generalized to the fourth isomorphism theorem, or the **correspondence theorem**, which further gives the correspondence between the subgroup lattice of a group and the subgroup lattice of a quotient group.

???+ note "Correspondence theorem"
    Let a group $G$ have a normal subgroup $N\trianglelefteq G$; then there exists a bijection $\varphi:\mathcal H\rightarrow\mathcal S$ between all subgroups of the group $G$ containing $N$, $\mathcal H=\{H:N\subseteq H\subseteq G\}$, and all subgroups of the quotient group $G/N$, $\mathcal S=\{S:S\le G/N\}$, which maps $H\in\mathcal H$ to $H/N\in\mathcal S$. This bijection preserves the inclusion relation of subgroups, and the normal subgroups of $G$ are always mapped to normal subgroups of $G/N$.

??? info "About the content of the isomorphism theorems"
    In different textbooks, the content and names of the isomorphism theorems of groups may differ. What is chosen here is one common version. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphism_theorems#Note_on_numbers_and_names) summarizes the differences in the content and names of the isomorphism theorems in common textbooks.

## Group action

The third method for understanding the structure of a given group is to examine the action of the group on a set.

For example, the space symmetry group of an equilateral triangle examined in this article is defined through the action of the elements of the group (i.e. symmetry operations) on the triangle. As another example, the definition of the symmetric group $S_M$ can be given through the action of its elements on the set $M$. The so-called action here means that each element of the group corresponds to a permutation on a set.

???+ abstract "Group action on a set"
    For a group $G$ and a set $X$ and a mapping $G\times X\rightarrow X$, denote the image of $(g,x)$ under this mapping as $g\cdot x$; if this mapping satisfies the conditions $g_1\cdot(g_2\cdot x)=(g_1g_2)\cdot x$ and $e\cdot x=x$ for all $g_1,g_2\in G$ and $x\in X$, then this mapping is called a **group action** of the group $G$ on the set $X$.

??? info "\"Left action\" and \"right action\""
    The definition of a group action adopted here is called a left action in some places[^group-action], because in the notation $g\cdot x$, the group element is written on the left side of the set element. Correspondingly, at this time when the composition of group elements $g_1g_2$ acts on the set, we need to first perform the action of $g_2$, then perform the action of $g_1$. Of course, we can also correspondingly define a right action, denoted $x\cdot g$. At this time, the composition order of group elements is opposite to the left action, i.e. $x\cdot (g_1g_2)=(x\cdot g_1)\cdot g_2$. The two differ only in notation and have no essential difference, so this article adopts the notation of the left action by default.

For a group action satisfying the above definition, we naturally have the following construction

$$
\begin{aligned}
\varphi:{\color{Maroon}{G}}\rightarrow {\color{Orchid}{S_X}}&\\
{\color{Maroon}{g}}\mapsto {\color{Orchid}{\varphi_g}}&: {\color{RoyalBlue}{X}}\rightarrow{\color{YellowGreen}{X}}\\
&\quad {\color{RoyalBlue}{x}}\mapsto {\color{YellowGreen}{g\cdot x}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This mapping corresponds each element $g$ in the group $G$ to a permutation $\varphi_g$ on the set $X$, and the permutation $\varphi_g$ maps the element $x$ to $g\cdot x$.

According to the definition, the bijection $\varphi_e$ corresponding to the identity element $e$ in the group is the identity mapping on $X$, and the mapping $\varphi_g$ corresponding to an element $g$ in the group and the mapping $\varphi_{g^{-1}}$ corresponding to its inverse element $g^{-1}$ are inverse mappings of each other (this also shows why $\varphi_g$ is always a bijection). We can verify that $\varphi_{g_1g_2}=\varphi_{g_1}\varphi_{g_2}$, i.e. $\varphi$ is a group homomorphism from the group $G$ to the group $S_X$.

This group homomorphism $\varphi$ is called the **permutation representation** of this group action, which maps the group $G$ to a certain permutation group.

???+ abstract "Permutation group"
    If a group $G$ is a subgroup of some symmetric group, then the group $G$ is called a **permutation group**.

The kernel of this group homomorphism is also called the kernel of this group action. If the kernel of this group homomorphism is trivial, i.e. this homomorphism is injective, then this group action is called **faithful**, i.e. the permutation representation of this action faithfully reflects the information of the group structure. At this time, the group $G$ is isomorphic to the permutation group obtained from the permutation representation.

???+ info "Notation"
    In the following text, for convenience of expression, we will omit the $\cdot$ notation in group actions.

### Orbit

A group action is a binary mapping. Fixing an element $g$ in the group, we can obtain a permutation $\varphi_g$ on the set. And if we fix an element $x$ on the set, we can obtain all possible results of the group's action on that element.

???+ abstract "Orbit"
    For the action of a group $G$ on a set $X$ and $x\in X$, the **orbit** of $x$ under the action of the group $G$ is the subset $Gx=\{gx:g\in G\}$.

???+ example "Example: the space symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    For example, if we consider the action of the group $\langle s\rangle\le D_6$ on the vertex set of the equilateral triangle, then the orbit of vertex $1$ is $\{1\}$, and the orbits of vertices $2$ and $3$ are $\{2,3\}$. But, the action of the group $\langle r\rangle\le D_6$ on the vertex set has only one orbit, i.e. the entire vertex set.

It is easy to prove that under the action of the group $G$, all orbits of the set $X$ constitute a partition of that set, denoted $X/G$. But unlike cosets, these orbits are not necessarily of equal length.

### Stabilizer

Under a group action, the orbit length of an element in the set depends on how many permutations corresponding to elements in the group have it as a fixed point.

???+ example "Example: the space symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    For example, the reason the orbit length of vertex $1$ under the action of the group $\langle s\rangle\le D_6$ is one is that all elements in the group map vertex $1$ to itself; and the orbit length of vertex $2$ is two, because only the identity element $e$ maps vertex $2$ to itself.

This inspires the following definition.

???+ abstract "Stabilizer"
    For the action of a group $G$ on a set $X$ and $x\in X$, the **stabilizer** of $x$ in the group $G$ is the subgroup $G_x=\{g\in G:gx=x\}$.

The kernel of a group action is the intersection of the stabilizers of all elements in the set.

???+ example "Example: the space symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    Consider the group action of the group $D_6$ on the vertex set; then the stabilizer of vertex $1$ is $\{e,s\}=\langle s\rangle$. This is a subgroup of $D_6$. Because $D_6$ can be partitioned into the left cosets $\langle s\rangle$, $r\langle s\rangle$, and $r^2\langle s\rangle$, it is easy to find that the result of each left coset acting on vertex $1$ is the same.

This example shows that the elements on the orbit all correspond one-to-one with the left cosets of the stabilizer. This shows the following result.

???+ note "Theorem"
    For the action of a group $G$ on a set $X$, the stabilizer $G_x$ of an element $x\in X$ is a subgroup of $G$, and there exists a bijection between the left cosets of the subgroup $G_x$ and the orbit $Gx$.

??? note "Proof"
    It suffices to verify that the mapping $gG_x\mapsto gx$ is a well-defined bijection.

Using Lagrange's theorem, we can connect the orbit length with the number of cosets of the stabilizer. This is the **orbit-stabilizer theorem**.

???+ note "Orbit-stabilizer theorem"
    For the action of a finite group $G$ on a set $X$ and $x\in X$, $|Gx|=[G:G_x]=|G|/|G_x|$.

We can verify this conclusion in the above example.

### Burnside's lemma

This lemma gives the formula for the number of orbits of a group action.

???+ note "Burnside's lemma"
    For the action of a group $G$ on a set $X$, the number of orbits equals the average number of fixed points of the permutations corresponding to each element in the group, i.e.
    
    $$
    |X/G| = \frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}|X^g|.
    $$
    
    Here, $X^g=\{x\in X:gx=x\}$ is the set of fixed points of the permutation corresponding to the element $g\in G$.

??? note "Proof"
    The proof of this theorem is very concise. Note that the number of orbits can be written as
    
    $$
    |X/G|=\sum_{o\in X/G}1=\sum_{x\in X}\frac{1}{|Gx|}=\frac1{|G|}\sum_{x\in X}|G_x|.
    $$
    
    The last equals sign is the corollary above; and the right side differs from what is to be proved only by a Fubini theorem, because the summation formulas in them are both counting the set $\{(g,x)\in G\times X:gx=x\}$, only that the right side sums over $g$ first, while the formula to be proved sums over $x$ first.

This theorem has many uses in combinatorics, and can be used to count the number of "essentially different" objects. For more examples and discussion, refer to [Pólya counting](../combinatorics/polya.md).

### Cayley's theorem

To study the structure of a group using group actions, we need to choose an appropriate set. In fact, the group itself is such a set. Therefore, next we consider two common group actions of a group on itself, and use them to analyze the structure of the group.

The first such group action is the **left multiplication** of the group on itself. Its permutation representation is as follows.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\varphi:G\rightarrow S_G&\\
g\mapsto \varphi_g&: G\rightarrow G\\
&\quad x\mapsto gx
\end{aligned}
$$

The left multiplication action of a group is necessarily faithful, because a group satisfies the cancellation law. So, by the fundamental theorem of group homomorphism, $G$ can be embedded in the symmetric group $S_G$. This means that every group is isomorphic to some permutation group[^cayley].

???+ note "Cayley's theorem"
    A group $G$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of the symmetric group $S_G$.

This group action has only one orbit, and moreover, the stabilizer of each element is $\{e\}$.

### Conjugation action

The second action of a group on itself is called the **conjugation action**. Its permutation representation is as follows.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\varphi:G\rightarrow S_G&\\
g\mapsto \varphi_g&: G\rightarrow G\\
&\quad x\mapsto gxg^{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

Under the conjugation action of a group, both the orbit and the stabilizer have special names.

???+ abstract "Conjugacy class"
    For a group $G$ and $g\in G$, the **conjugacy class** of the element $g$ in the group $G$ is the orbit of $g$ under the conjugation action. If elements $g$ and $h$ are in the same conjugacy class, then $g$ and $h$ are said to be **conjugate**.

???+ abstract "Centralizer"
    For a group $G$ and $a\in G$, the **centralizer** of the element $a$ in the group $G$ is $C_G(a)=\{g\in G:ga=ag\}$.

???+ abstract "Center"
    For a group $G$, the **center** of the group $G$ is $Z(G)=\cap_{a\in G}C_G(a)=\{g\in G:\forall a\in G(ga=ag)\}$.

The center of a group is the set of elements that commute with all elements in the group; because it is the kernel of a group action, it must be a normal subgroup. The size of the center of a group indicates the gap between it and a commutative group. The centralizer of a given element is the set of all elements that commute with that element; it is also the largest among all subgroups whose center includes it, which is the source of its name; at the same time, because it is the stabilizer of some element under the conjugation action, it is a subgroup. A conjugacy class is generally not a subgroup.

???+ example "Example: the space symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    Returning to the group $D_6$, its center is $\{e\}\neq G$, which shows that it is not a commutative group. The centralizer $C_G(r)$ of the element $r$ is $\langle r\rangle$, and the centralizer $C_G(s)$ of the element $s$ is $\langle s\rangle$. In general, for all $g\in G$, $\langle g\rangle\le C_G(g)$ always holds. The group $D_6$ has three conjugacy classes, i.e. $\{e\},\{r,r^2\},\{s,sr,sr^2\}$. It is easy to find that conjugate elements are all of the same order[^conjugate].

A group is partitioned into several conjugacy classes under the conjugation action. So, we can write the **class equation**.

???+ note "Class equation"
    For a group $G$, let $\{\mathcal K_i\}_{i=1}^r$ be all conjugacy classes of length greater than one, and $g_i$ be a representative element of $\mathcal K_i$; then
    
    $$
    |G|=|Z(G)|+\sum_{i=1}^r[G:C_G(g_i)].
    $$

It can be used to analyze the structure of a group, for example to prove the [Sylow theorems](#sylow-theorems) below.

### Normalizer and centralizer

In fact, the power set of a group can also be the object of a group action. Here we focus on the conjugation action of a group on all its subsets $X=\mathcal P(G)$. Its permutation representation is as follows.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\varphi:G\rightarrow S_X&\\
g\mapsto \varphi_g&: X\rightarrow X\\
&\quad S\mapsto gSg^{-1} = \{gsg^{-1}:s\in S\}
\end{aligned}
$$

Under this conjugation action, we can likewise define the conjugacy class of subsets of the group. Its kernel is still the center of the group. Its stabilizer is called the normalizer.

???+ abstract "Normalizer"
    For a group $G$ and its subset $S\subseteq G$, the **normalizer** of the subset $S$ in the group $G$ is $N_G(S)=\{g\in G:gSg^{-1}=S\}$.

The normalizer is the stabilizer of $S$ under the conjugation action, so it must be a subgroup of $G$; the normalizer of $S$ is the largest among the subgroups that can make $S$ contained in their normal subgroup, which is also the source of the name normalizer. In particular, for a singleton set $\{a\}$, $N_G(\{a\})=C_G(a)$.

The centralizer can also be similarly generalized to the case of subsets.

???+ abstract "Centralizer"
    For a group $G$ and its subset $S\subseteq G$, the **centralizer** of the subset $S$ in the group $G$ is $C_G(S)=\{g\in G:\forall s\in S(gsg^{-1}=s)\}$.

The centralizer is actually the kernel of the conjugation action of the group $N_G(S)$ on $S$. So, we must have $C_G(S)\trianglelefteq N_G(S)\le G$, so by the transitivity of subgroups, the centralizer is also a subgroup. In particular, the center of the group $Z(G)=C_G(G)\trianglelefteq N_G(G)=G$ must be a normal subgroup.

### Sylow theorems

Furthermore, analyzing the conjugation action of a finite group, we can obtain the Sylow theorems. They are a powerful tool for handling the structure of finite groups, and can quickly obtain the structure of a large number of small-order groups.

???+ abstract "$p$-group"
    For a group $G$, if there exist a prime $p$ and a positive integer $\alpha$ such that $|G|=p^\alpha$, then it is called a **$p$-group**.

??? info "About the definition of a $p$-group"
    A $p$-group has another definition, i.e. a group in which the order of all elements is a power of a prime. These two definitions are equivalent for finite groups; but, the second definition also applies to the case of infinite groups. In addition, regarding the definition of a $p$-group, different references may disagree on whether $\{e\}$ counts as a $p$-group; readers should distinguish when reading.

???+ abstract "$p$-subgroup"
    For a group $G$ and its subgroup $P\le G$, if $P$ itself is a $p$-group, then $P$ is called a **$p$-subgroup**.

???+ note "Cauchy's theorem"
    If a prime $p$ can divide the order of a group $G$, then there must exist an element of order $p$.

Cauchy's theorem guarantees the existence of $p$-subgroups. In fact, through a more detailed analysis, we can obtain a stronger conclusion, i.e. the existence of Sylow $p$-subgroups.

???+ abstract "Sylow $p$-subgroup"
    For a group $G$ and its subgroup $P\le G$, if $|G|=p^\alpha m$, $p\perp m$, and $|P|=p^\alpha$, then $P$ is called a **Sylow $p$-subgroup**.

That is to say, a Sylow $p$-subgroup is a maximal $p$-subgroup. The Sylow theorems assert the existence of Sylow $p$-subgroups, which provides a partial converse of Lagrange's theorem.

???+ note "Sylow theorems"
    Let the order $|G|$ of a finite group $G$ be expressible in the form $p^\alpha m$, where $p$ is prime and $p$ cannot divide $m$; then we have the following conclusions:
    
    1.  There exists a Sylow $p$-subgroup;
    2.  For a Sylow $p$-subgroup $P$ and a $p$-subgroup $Q$ of the group $G$, there exists $g\in G$ such that $Q\le gPg^{-1}$; in particular, all Sylow $p$-subgroups are conjugate;
    3.  The number $n_p$ of Sylow $p$-subgroups in the group $G$ satisfies $n_p\equiv 1\pmod p$, $n_p\mid m$, and $n_p=[G:N_G(P)]$, where $P$ is any Sylow $p$-subgroup.

??? note "Proof"
    To prove the first part, induct on $|G|$. Consider the class equation
    
    $$
    |G|=|Z(G)|+\sum_{i=1}^r[G:C_G(g_i)].
    $$
    
    If $p\mid|Z(G)|$, then we can take any $p$-order subgroup $N\le Z(G)$ of the center; then we must have $N\trianglelefteq Z(G)\trianglelefteq G$, so $G/N$ is a smaller group. According to the induction hypothesis, it has a Sylow $p$-subgroup $P/N$; then the corresponding preimage $P$ under the natural homomorphism is a Sylow $p$-subgroup of the original group $G$. So, we only need to consider the case where $p$ cannot divide $|Z(G)|$. At this time, we can take $g_i\in G$ such that $p$ cannot divide $[G:C_G(g_i)]$, so all powers of $p$ in $|G|$ appear in $|C_G(g_i)|$. Again according to the induction hypothesis, $|C_G(g_i)|$ has a Sylow $p$-subgroup $P$, which is exactly a Sylow $p$-subgroup of the original group $G$.
    
    To prove the second and third parts, let the conjugacy class of a Sylow $p$-subgroup $P$ of the group $G$ be $\mathcal S=\{gPg^{-1}:g\in G\}$, and examine the result of restricting the conjugation action of the group $G$ on $\mathcal S$ to a certain $p$-subgroup $Q$. The set $\mathcal S$ can be partitioned into several orbits under this action. Let there be $r$ orbits in total, with representative element $P_i$ for each orbit; then we have the identity
    
    $$
    |\mathcal S|=\sum_{i=1}^r[Q:Q\cap N_G(P_i)].
    $$
    
    Here, $Q\cap N_G(P_i)$ is the stabilizer of $P_i$ under the above group action. Let $H=Q\cap N_G(P_i)$; then obviously $H\le N_G(P_i)$, so by the second isomorphism theorem of groups, $P_iH/P_i\cong H/(P_i\cap H)$. So, $|P_iH|=|P_i||H|/|P_i\cap H|$, and the right side is a power of $p$, so the left side must also be a power of $p$, and $|P_i|\le |P_iH|$ must hold. But, $P_i$ is already the subgroup of $G$ with the largest power of $p$, so $P_i=P_iH$. Substituting into the previous isomorphism relation, we have $H=P_i\cap H=Q\cap P_i$. Here we use the obvious relation $P_i\le N_G(P_i)$. This way, we know the stabilizer $Q\cap N_G(P_i)$ is actually $Q\cap P_i$. So, the above formula can be written as
    
    $$
    |\mathcal S|=\sum_{i=1}^r[Q:Q\cap P_i].
    $$
    
    This equality holds for all $p$-subgroups $Q$.
    
    Naturally, the Sylow $p$-subgroup $P$ is also a $p$-subgroup. Substituting $P$ for $Q$ in the above formula, then the right side has one and only one term whose size is $1$, and the rest are all multiples of $p$. Therefore, the size $|\mathcal S|$ of the conjugacy class of the Sylow $p$-subgroup $P$ must be $1$ modulo $p$. At the same time, because this formula holds for any $p$-subgroup $Q$, there naturally exists $g\in G$ such that $Q\le gPg^{-1}$; otherwise, for all $P_i$ in the above equality $|Q\cap P_i|<|Q|$, which contradicts $|\mathcal S|\equiv 1\pmod p$. If at this time we further consider $Q$ to be some Sylow $p$-subgroup different from $P$, then we must have $Q=gPg^{-1}$ for some $g\in G$, i.e. all Sylow $p$-subgroups are conjugate. Therefore, the number $n_p$ of Sylow $p$-subgroups is exactly $|\mathcal S|$, which must satisfy $n_p\equiv 1\pmod p$. Finally, noting that $n_p=|\mathcal S|=[G:N_G(P)]$ and $P\le N_G(P)$, we have $n_p\mid m$. This completely proves the second and third parts of the theorem.

??? example "Application: up to isomorphism, groups of order six are only $C_6$ and $S_3$"
    Let the order of $G$ be $6$. Then, according to the Sylow theorems, it has a Sylow $2$-subgroup, and its number satisfies $n_2\equiv 1\pmod 2$ and $n_2\mid 3$, so there are only two cases: $n_2=1$ or $n_2=3$. Similarly, we can prove that the group $G$ has one and only one Sylow $3$-subgroup, i.e. $n_3=1$.
    
    For the case $n_2=1$, we can find that the group $G$ has one Sylow $2$-subgroup, so it has one element of order $2$; and it has one Sylow $3$-subgroup, so it has two elements of order $3$. The group $G$ also has an identity element, and for the remaining elements, according to Lagrange's theorem, their order must divide $6$. And it cannot be a new element of order $2$ or $3$, otherwise a new Sylow $p$-subgroup different from the previous ones would appear; so, the remaining elements can only be elements of order $6$. The existence of an element with the same order as the group means that the group $G$ is a cyclic group, so $G\cong C_6$.
    
    For the case $n_2=3$, the group $G$ has three conjugate Sylow $3$-subgroups. Consider the conjugation action of the group $G$ on these three Sylow $3$-subgroups. For any Sylow $3$-subgroup $P$, according to the Sylow theorems, $|N_G(P)|=2$; but also $P\le N_G(P)$, so $P=N_G(P)$. Therefore, the intersection of the normalizers of these three Sylow $3$-subgroups, i.e. the kernel of this conjugation action, is trivial. So, this action is faithful; it embeds $G$ into the permutation group $S_3$ on these three Sylow $3$-subgroups. But, because $|G|=|S_3|$, we must have $G\cong S_3$.

## Finitely generated Abelian groups

After mastering the basic tools for analyzing group structure, now we focus on the structure of a class of groups.

As mentioned in the overview, Abelian groups, because elements can commute, have a simpler structure compared with other groups. Especially simple are those Abelian groups that can be generated by finitely many elements.

???+ abstract "Finitely generated"
    If a group $G$ has a finite generating set, then the group $G$ is called **finitely generated**.

The classification theorem of this section shows that a finitely generated Abelian group can be regarded as a simple combination of finitely many cyclic groups. The groups involved in algorithm competitions are mostly finite groups. A finite Abelian group is necessarily finitely generated, so this conclusion always applies.

### Direct product

The previous analysis of groups mainly focused on how to decompose a group into smaller groups; conversely, we can naturally discuss how to combine two groups into a larger group. Among all possible ways of combination, the direct product of groups is the simplest.

The basic idea of the direct product of groups is that, given two groups $G$ and $H$, consider their Cartesian product $G\times H$; the operation of the pair $(g,h)$ is defined as operating on the two components separately, with the components not affecting each other. The result obtained this way is obviously a larger group, and the original two groups can be trivially embedded in the new group.

???+ abstract "Direct product"
    The **direct product** of a group $(G,\cdot_G)$ and a group $(H,\cdot_H)$ is the group $(G\times H,\cdot)$, where the binary operation $\cdot:(G\times H)\times(G\times H)\rightarrow G\times H$ is defined as $(g_1,h_1)\cdot(g_2,h_2)=(g_1\cdot_G g_2,h_1\cdot_H h_2)$. The direct product of the group $G$ and the group $H$ is denoted $G\times H$.

For the direct product $G\times H$, we trivially have the embedding mappings $g\mapsto(g,e_H)$ and $h\mapsto(e_G,h)$. Conversely, the mappings $(g,h)\mapsto g$ and $(g,h)\mapsto h$ are group homomorphisms, whose kernels are $\{e_G\}\times H$ and $G\times\{e_H\}$ respectively. These two kernels are exactly the images of the above embedding mappings, and their intersection is trivial, i.e. $\{(e_G,e_H)\}$. So, in the direct product $G\times H$ there are two subgroups, both normal subgroups, whose intersection is trivial, and whose product is the direct product $G\times H$ itself.

Such an analysis actually gives a necessary and sufficient condition for a group to be written as the direct product of its two subgroups.

???+ note "Theorem"
    For a group $G$ and its subgroups $H_1,H_2\le G$, $G\cong H_1\times H_2$ if and only if $H_1,H_2\trianglelefteq G$, $H_1\cap H_2=\{e\}$, and $G=H_1H_2$.

??? note "Proof"
    The necessity of these conditions has been discussed in the main text; here we prove their sufficiency. Examine the mapping $\varphi:G\rightarrow H_1\times H_2$ satisfying $h_1h_2\mapsto(h_1,h_2)$. The mapping $\varphi$ is well-defined, because for any $h_1,k_1\in H_1$ and $h_2,k_2\in H_2$, $h_1h_2=k_1k_2$ means $h_1=k_1$ and $h_2=k_2$; this is because $k_1^{-1}h_1=k_2h_2^{-1}\in H_1\cap H_2=\{e\}$. To show that $\varphi$ is a group homomorphism, we need to show that $(h_1h_2)(k_1k_2)=h_1k_1h_2k_2$, which is equivalent to $k_1$ and $h_2$ being commutable, i.e. $k_1h_2k_1^{-1}h_2^{-1}=e$. To prove this relation, we only need to note that $k_1h_2k_1^{-1}h_2^{-1}=(k_1h_2k_1^{-1})h_2^{-1}\in (k_1H_2k_1^{-1})H_2=H_2$, and similarly $k_1(h_2k_1^{-1}h_2^{-1})\in H_1$, so $k_1h_2k_1^{-1}h_2^{-1}\in H_1\cap H_2=\{e\}$. These prove that $\varphi$ is a group homomorphism. It is obviously a bijection, so it is an isomorphism, i.e. $G\cong H_1\times H_2$.

In a direct product, the elements of the two direct product factors are necessarily commutable, because $hg=(e_G,h)(g,e_H)=(g,h)=gh$. So, if both direct product factors are Abelian groups, then the direct product is necessarily also an Abelian group.

Not all groups can be written as the direct product of two non-trivial subgroups.

???+ example "Example: the space symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    For example, the group $D_6=\langle r,s\rangle$ is not isomorphic to $\langle r\rangle\times\langle s\rangle$, because as the direct product of two cyclic groups, the latter must be an Abelian group.

The following classification theorem shows that a finitely generated Abelian group can be written as the direct product of finitely many cyclic groups.

### Classification theorem

For finitely generated Abelian groups, we have the following classification theorem. It is called the **fundamental theorem of finitely generated Abelian groups**.

???+ note "Fundamental theorem of finitely generated Abelian groups"
    For a finitely generated Abelian group $G$, there exist an integer $r\ge0$ and $n_1,\cdots,n_s\ge 2$ such that
    
    $$
    G\cong C_\infty^r\times C_{n_1}\times\cdots\times C_{n_s}.
    $$
    
    In particular, $r$ is uniquely determined, called the **rank** of the group $G$, and moreover
    
    -   we can choose integers $n_1,\cdots,n_s$ so that they satisfy $n_1\ge2,\ n_1|n_2,\ \cdots,\ n_{s-1}|n_s$; at this time, the integers $n_1,\cdots,n_s$ are uniquely determined, and the factors $C_{n_i}$ are called the **invariant factors** of the group $G$;
    -   we can also choose integers $n_1,\cdots,n_s$ so that they are all in the form of prime powers; at this time, these prime powers are also all uniquely determined, and the factors $C_{n_i}$ are called the **elementary divisors** of the group $G$.

The theorem first asserts that a finitely generated Abelian group is necessarily the direct product of finitely many cyclic groups.

??? note "Proof"
    Here we give a proof with a simple form[^proof-abelian]; a more profound proof should refer to the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain[^module-pid]. In the proof, for brevity of writing, we will use additive notation instead of the general multiplicative notation in a group; at this time $0$ represents the identity element, and the notation $mx$ represents the $m$-th power of $x$.
    
    Let $G$ be minimally generated by $k$ elements. The proof of the theorem needs to induct on $k$. When $k=1$ the conclusion is trivial. When $k>1$, take the one with the smallest order of $x_1$ among all generator tuples $\langle x_1,x_2,\cdots,x_k\rangle$ of $G$. Below we want to show $G=\langle x_1\rangle\times\langle x_2,\cdots,x_k\rangle$; the latter, according to the induction hypothesis, can already be decomposed into the direct product of $(k-1)$ cyclic groups, so the induction step is proved.
    
    According to the characterization of the direct product of groups, if the direct product decomposition does not hold, there must exist a relation $m_1x_1+m_2x_2+\cdots+m_kx_k=0$ with $m_1x_1\neq 0$. For negative coefficients $m_i$, we can replace $x_i$ with the inverse element $-x_i$, then all coefficients $m_i$ can be taken as non-negative integers. Moreover, at this time we can take $0< m_1 <|x_1|$. If we further take $d=\gcd(m_1,m_2,\cdots,m_k)$ and $c_i=m_i/d$, then $y_1=c_1x_1+c_2x_2+\cdots+c_kx_k$ must satisfy $dy_1=0$, so $|y_1|\le d\le m_1< |x_1|$, i.e. $y_1$ is an element of smaller order than $x_1$.
    
    Below we prove that $y_1$ can be extended into a set of generators of $G$. That is to say, there exist elements $y_2,\cdots,y_k\in G$ satisfying $G=\langle y_1,y_2,\cdots,y_k\rangle$. The only known condition here is that $y_1$ itself can be written as $c_1x_1+c_2x_2+\cdots+c_kx_k$, where the coefficients $c_i$ are all natural numbers and their greatest common divisor is one. Without loss of generality, assume the coefficients are arranged in (non-strictly) decreasing order; then $y_1$ can also be written as $(c_1-c_2)x_1+c_2(x_1+x_2)+\cdots+c_kx_k$. At this time, comparing with the previous condition, we can find that the coefficients are still all natural numbers, and the greatest common divisor is one, and $G=\langle x_1,x_1+x_2,x_3,\cdots,x_k\rangle$, but the sum of all coefficients decreased by $c_2$. If $c_2=0$, then necessarily $c_1=1$, and the conclusion is trivial; otherwise, the sum of coefficients strictly decreased. This means that if we induct on the sum of coefficients, we can prove that $y_1$ satisfying the above condition can always be extended into a set of generators of $G$.
    
    Furthermore, at this time we have found a generator $y_1$ of smaller order than $x_1$, which contradicts the choice of $x_1$. So, the direct product decomposition must hold, and according to the induction principle we know the conclusion to be proved holds.

Of course, a cyclic group may be of infinite order or finite order; they are respectively the $C_\infty$ part and the $C_{n_i}$ part of the above decomposition. Then, the theorem gives the structure of the finite-order cyclic group $C_n$. The part of the theorem about the elementary divisor decomposition actually relies on the following observation.

???+ note "Lemma"
    If $m$ and $n$ are coprime, then $C_{mn}\cong C_m\times C_n$.

??? note "Proof"
    Let $x$ and $y$ be generators of $C_m$ and $C_n$ respectively; then because $(m,n)=1$, we have $|(x,y)|=mn=|C_m\times C_n|$. So, $(x,y)$ is a generator of $C_m\times C_n$, i.e. $C_m\times C_n=\langle(x,y)\rangle$. So, it is a cyclic group of order $mn$, which must be isomorphic to $C_{mn}$.

Given a cyclic group $C_{n}$, if according to the fundamental theorem of arithmetic $n=p_1^{r_1}\cdots p_k^{r_k}$, then repeatedly using the lemma, we can prove $C_n=C_{p_1^{r_1}}\times\cdots\times C_{p_k^{r_k}}$. After these two steps of decomposition, we have actually already obtained the elementary divisor decomposition in the theorem. Then according to the lemma, recombining these prime-power-order cyclic groups so that their orders meet the requirements, we can obtain the invariant factor decomposition in the theorem. The uniqueness in the theorem can be proved by induction.

??? example "Example: classification of Abelian groups of order 24"
    As an example, through the theorem we know that there are three kinds of Abelian groups of order 24 in total, listed as follows.

    | Invariant factor decomposition | Elementary divisor decomposition |
    | :-----------------------: | :---------------------------------: |
    | $C_{24}$ | $C_{3}\times C_{8}$ |
    | $C_{2}\times C_{12}$ | $C_2\times C_{3}\times C_4$ |
    | $C_2\times C_2\times C_6$ | $C_2\times C_2\times C_2\times C_3$ |
